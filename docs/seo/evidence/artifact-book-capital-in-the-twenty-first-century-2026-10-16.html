Capital in the Twenty-First Century is a major study of wealth and income inequality. Using historical evidence from several countries, Thomas Piketty examines how ownership of property, businesses, investments and inherited wealth becomes concentrated over time. His best-known argument is expressed as r > g: when the average return on capital is greater than the rate of economic growth, accumulated wealth can increase faster than wages and national income. This may allow already wealthy families to gain a progressively larger share of society’s resources. Piketty supports progressive taxation, transparency and international cooperation, although economists continue to debate parts of his evidence and policy proposals.

  1. Wealth and income are different: Income is received over time, while wealth consists of accumulated assets such as land, houses, businesses and investments.
  1. Capital can grow faster than wages: Owners of valuable assets may become wealthier even when ordinary salaries rise slowly.
  1. The formula r > g summarizes the argument: Returns on capital can exceed the overall growth rate of the economy.
  1. Inheritance can reproduce inequality: Large fortunes may pass across generations, giving some people major advantages from birth.
  1. High growth can temporarily reduce inequality: Rapid economic expansion may increase wages and create opportunities for people without inherited wealth.
  1. Slow growth can strengthen inherited wealth: When economies grow slowly, existing capital becomes relatively more important.
  1. Inequality is shaped by public policy: Taxation, education, labour laws, wars and political decisions influence the distribution of wealth.
  1. Transparency is necessary: Reliable information about income, property and financial assets is required to understand inequality.
  1. Extreme inequality may affect democracy: Very wealthy individuals can acquire disproportionate political and social influence.
  1. Study-note wording (edition unverified): “When the rate of return on capital exceeds the rate of growth of output and income…”
