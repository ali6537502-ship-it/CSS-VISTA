When the Pacific Breathes Fire, Pakistan Burns

By Ali Hassan Sargana · April 28, 2026 · Read text

Editorial clarification · October 15, 2026

Ambient temperature and wet-bulb temperature are different

The statistics panel links a reported 51°C ambient-air temperature to a wet-bulb survival threshold. An ambient-air temperature alone does not establish a wet-bulb reading or show that a human survival threshold was crossed. NOAA’s National Weather Service distinguishes dry-bulb air temperature from wet-bulb temperature and explains that their difference depends on humidity. This clarification corrects that inference; it does not independently verify the reported Jacobabad temperature or the article’s other figures. The original article and images are preserved below.

  • NOAA National Weather Service: Dry Bulb, Wet Bulb, and Dew Point Temperatures

Editorial clarification · October 15, 2026

What the climate studies measure

A 2019 study matching the 0.18°C figure examined 15 stations in Pakistan’s northern monsoon belt and reported that value for 2010–2017. It does not measure 2018–2026 or establish a continuing nationwide annual trend. The FAO–WMO report published in 2026 does discuss a fruit-productivity decline of up to 50% in Box 10 on rural Punjab. That box draws on earlier research, including Habib et al. (2022); it is a case-study finding, not a new nationwide 2026 measurement or a uniform forecast for every farm.

  • Safdar et al. (2019): study abstract and reference period
  • FAO–WMO (2026): Extreme heat and agriculture

Original archived text

El Niño is returning. The PMD has confirmed it. The data is damning. And yet Pakistan, a country that has endured 51°C summers, half-a-million displaced by floods, and hospitals overwhelmed with heatstroke patients remains perilously unprepared for what is coming.

It was May of 2022 when Jacobabad made headlines the world did not want to read. The thermometer touched 51°C. Scientists from Cambridge published a paper that same month observing that parts of the Indus Valley were crossing the "wet-bulb temperature" threshold the point at which the human body, even at rest, even in the shade, cannot cool itself fast enough to survive. That was not a warning from the future. That was a dispatch from a future that had already arrived, silently, in a city most of the world could not locate on a map.

Now, as April 2026 draws to a close, the Pakistan Meteorological Department has issued what should be read as a five-alarm emergency not merely a weather advisory. El Niño conditions are likely to emerge between May and July of this year, the PMD warned just days ago. The same force that made 2023 the second-hottest year in recorded history, and 2024 the all-time record-setter, is preparing to re-enter. This time, it arrives in a country whose defences against heat institutional, infrastructural, medical remain perilously thin.

Understanding El Niño is essential to understanding what is coming. It is not a storm or a cyclone things we can track, brace for, evacuate from. It is a planetary-scale warming of surface waters in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific that reshapes weather patterns across continents. It suppresses monsoon rains across South Asia. It amplifies the baking summer highs that already strain Pakistan's south. And when it combines with the background warming driven by decades of unchecked carbon emissions, it does not merely push temperatures upward. It compresses the seasons. It turns a heatwave that would once last three days into one that lasts three weeks.

The numbers from 2025 alone should cause sleepless nights in Islamabad. Jacobabad hit 50°C last June. Bhakkar and Dadu recorded 49°C. During April's heatwave, temperatures across southern Punjab and Sindh ran six to eight degrees Celsius above normal. The PMD has itself acknowledged that these heat-prone regions south Punjab, upper Sindh, southern Balochistan can touch 52°C under heatwave conditions. And current readings, even before El Niño takes hold, are already two to four degrees above the seasonal norm. The trajectory is unambiguous.

This is not fearmongering. Researchers monitoring Pakistan's climate have documented that since 2010, temperatures across the country's monsoon belt have been rising at 0.18°C per year. That sounds modest until you recall the physics: for every degree of atmospheric warming, the air's moisture-holding capacity increases by seven per cent. This creates a catastrophic whipsaw searing, rainless heat followed by violent, concentrated downpours. The 2025 data proved this pattern in blood and broken homes: floods claimed over a thousand lives, displaced some six million people, and shaved an estimated half a percentage point off national GDP.

The consequences of the coming heat are not abstract. A joint assessment released this week by the World Meteorological Organisation and the Food and Agriculture Organisation found that heatwaves in Punjab's rain-fed zones have already driven fruit productivity down by as much as 50 per cent in some areas. Livestock face increased disease outbreaks and sharply reduced milk yields. Heat stress causes large ruminants to produce less; smaller ruminants face surging rates of foot-and-mouth disease. The problem is compounded by the accelerated spoilage of perishables vegetables, dairy which reduces food availability and drives up local prices.

There is also the spectre of the electrical grid. Every heatwave brings power outages. And every power outage during a heatwave turns ordinary homes into death traps for the most vulnerable. The paradox is excruciating and familiar: people need cooling to survive the heat; the heat spikes power demand beyond the grid's capacity; the grid fails; people die. In June 2024, health officials reported nearly 700 deaths in Sindh during a single severe heatwave, most of them in Karachi. This cycle is not new. But with El Niño bearing down and the NDMA already warning of intense conditions across multiple regions, it threatens to play out at a scale we have not witnessed before.

What must be said, bluntly, is this: Pakistan did not cause this crisis. Our share of global carbon emissions is less than one per cent. And yet we consistently rank among the top ten countries most vulnerable to climate impacts. We are the case study the world needs to study, and the constituency it consistently neglects. The climate finance promised by wealthier nations those whose industrial histories created this atmospheric debt has arrived in trickles where rivers were needed. That injustice is real and must be named. But naming it cannot become an excuse for the state's own failures.

Our cities continue to devour green cover and replace it with concrete, worsening urban heat islands. Our drainage systems already wholly inadequate will be overwhelmed when the rains finally arrive after weeks of baking ground. Heatwave response plans exist largely on paper. The cooling centres are often unopen, the advisories untranslated for the communities that most need them, the hospitals understocked for the surges that are now predictable with near-mathematical certainty.

What the El Niño forecast demands is not panic. It demands a seriousness of purpose commensurate with the scale of the threat. NDMA and provincial disaster management authorities must move from issuing advisories to enforcing emergency preparedness verifying cooling centres are operational, supply chains for oral rehydration salts are stocked, and heat-vulnerable populations are mapped and contacted before the crisis arrives. School closures must be decided ahead of time, not in the middle of the emergency. Farmers in south Punjab and upper Sindh must receive targeted advisories about crop management, water rationing, and livestock protection.

The Pacific is warming. The data is unambiguous. The window to prepare is narrow, and it is closing. The question is whether we will act with the urgency this demands, or wait, once again, for the disaster to force our hand. If past behaviour is the guide, we know the answer. But past behaviour has also shown us what that answer costs in lives, in livelihoods, in a country that deserves far better than to be the world's sacrificial example of climate injustice.

The heat in numbers

  • 51°C - Jacobabad peak, May 2022 crossed wet-bulb survival threshold
  • +8°C - Above-normal temp forecast, South Punjab PMD 2026
  • 41% - Rainfall deficit Q1 2025, nationwide PMD Drought Bulletin
  • 50% - Drop in fruit productivity, Punjab rain-fed zones WMO/FAO 2026
  • <1% - Pakistan's global carbon share yet top-10 most climate-vulnerable
  • 700 - Deaths in Sindh from single heatwave, June 2024

When Eid Turns to Ash - the Chaman Phatak Bombing

By Ali Hassan Sargana · May 25, 2026 · Read text

Editorial clarification · October 15, 2026

The article contains an unresolved day and date inconsistency

The first archive image is dated Monday, 25 May 2026. The article places the attack on May 24 and refers to a technique not used “before Saturday”. In the Gregorian calendar, 24 May 2026 was Sunday. These day and date references are inconsistent. The original wording remains below; the intended event date, day and casualty account require the author’s reporting sources. This clarification does not assign a different event date.

Original archived text

The Chaman Phatak bombing was not merely an act of terror. It was a mirror held up to a state that keeps promising security overhauls and keeps burying the same promises alongside its dead.

There is a particular cruelty to a bombing on the eve of Eid. The mother folding a child’s clothes into a too-large bag; the soldier rehearsing the moment he will walk into his courtyard after months away; the elder sister who has waited for the train because waiting is what she does best, these are the lives the men of the Majeed Brigade studied before they decided where to park their explosive-laden vehicle at Chaman Phatak.

On May 24, a shuttle train carrying personnel from the Quetta cantonment toward the Jaffar Express was turned into a furnace. By the time the smoke had lifted on the morning of the 25th, the toll had crossed two dozen dead and the wards of Civil Hospital Quetta had been placed under emergency. Two carriages overturned. Homes along the line, whose residents had no part in any quarrel between the state and its insurgents, were caved in. The prime minister condemned the attack as a “cowardly act of terrorism”, a formula so worn by use that the words seemed to exhaust themselves before they reached the bereaved.

The condemnation is correct. It is also, by itself, useless.

Consider the tactical signature. The Balochistan Liberation Army has, over a quarter of a century, refined an inventory of methods: pressure-plate IEDs along the Bolan curves, sabotage of gas pipelines in Dera Bugti, targeted shootings of non-Baloch labourers in Turbat. The Majeed Brigade, its suicide cadre, has executed station-front bombings, hotel sieges, the assault on the Karachi Stock Exchange, the strike on the Chinese consulate. What it had not done before Saturday was drive a vehicle-borne explosive directly into a moving train carriage. This is not theatre; it is doctrine. The decision to use a VBIED to maximise structural kinetic damage on a target in motion is a technique the world has not seen the BLA borrow at this scale, and it tells us something important: the brigade is learning, refining, and importing.

There is a second signature, less visible but more damning. The shuttle in question originates inside the Quetta cantonment, which is to say it begins its life under the protection of a fortified perimeter. To strike it at Chaman Phatak, a crossing barely beyond the wire, required surveillance of military movement, identification of a choke point, timing aligned with departure, and the patience to wait. None of this is amateur work. It is the work of the Zirab, the BLA’s intelligence wing, and it presumes a degree of local cover that no insurgency can sustain without something approaching tolerance, or fear, in the surrounding population. We may dislike that sentence. It remains true.

I have been thinking about that crossing, Chaman Phatak, a name no one in Lahore or Karachi would have recognised yesterday, and what it now joins. In November 2024, a suicide bomber detonated himself on the platform of the Quetta railway station, killing more than two dozen people. Authorities promised a comprehensive overhaul of rail security. Less than eighteen months later, the same line, the same kind of target, the same week of festive travel. The overhaul, whatever it consisted of, did not include the simple imagination required to ask: what happens when the train leaves the platform?

This is the recurring failure of our security thinking. We harden the gate and forget the road.

Set this against the strategic horizon. Pakistan is, at this moment, in formal discussion with China over a second phase of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor. Beijing has spent two years quietly raising the temperature of its private remonstrations on the safety of its nationals and projects in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Every successful BLA operation against a high-profile target, a train carrying soldiers, a convoy headed to Gwadar, a hotel hosting foreign engineers, strengthens the hand of those in Beijing who argue that Pakistan’s western corridor cannot absorb additional Chinese commitment until its house is in order. The BLA understands this. The choice of a train, with its photographable wreckage and unmistakable symbolism of state penetration into Baloch geography, is not incidental.

What of the response? If history is any guide, the coming days will bring a flurry of intelligence-based operations across Mastung, Kalat, Awaran and Panjgur. A handful of “high-value targets” will be neutralised; a press release will be issued; ministers will pay ceremonial visits to the cantonment. None of it will address the question that ought to keep us awake at night: why does a movement the state declared defeated in 2014 keep recruiting?

The honest answer is uncomfortable. The Majeed Brigade is no longer the project of disaffected tribal sardars; it draws its bombers from university classrooms, from the children of teachers and small contractors, from the very middle class that two decades of development rhetoric was supposed to integrate. Bashir Zeb, the current BLA leadership, is not a Marri prince. The constituency has shifted. The radicalisation is, in part, intellectual, and you cannot kill an idea with a Cobra helicopter.

This is the conversation Islamabad has refused to have for twenty years. Enforced disappearances continue. The Voice for Baloch Missing Persons continues its sit-ins. The names of the disappeared continue to be the most reliable recruitment poster the BLA has ever produced. Until the federation is willing to address the political grievance, to put genuine devolution, transparent royalty distribution, and accountability for the security apparatus on the table, every kinetic gain will be erased by the next graduating class.

What should be done to prevent future chaos?

Diagnosis without prescription is its own form of abdication. If the state is serious about breaking the cycle that Chaman Phatak has once again exposed, it must move on at least five fronts simultaneously, and it must move on all of them, because the BLA’s strength lies precisely in the federation’s habit of addressing one dimension while leaving the others untouched.

First, a genuine security audit of critical infrastructure. The November 2024 overhaul of Quetta railway station security failed because it was a perimeter response to a perimeter problem. The state must now commission an independent, time-bound review of all rail corridors in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, not a departmental self-assessment, but one conducted with external technical expertise and binding timelines. Every choke point, every crossing, every stretch where a train slows must be mapped and graded for vulnerability. The results should be tabled before a parliamentary committee and acted on within ninety days, not archived.

Second, an enforceable moratorium on enforced disappearances. The Commission of Inquiry on Enforced Disappearances has submitted thousands of cases over seventeen years; the families still wait. The state must move from commission to court: every pending case should be heard by a dedicated bench with a six-month disposal mandate, the families should be notified of any finding, and any officer found to have ordered an illegal detention should face suspension pending inquiry. This is not soft on terror. This is removing the single most effective recruiting instrument the BLA possesses.

Third, transparent royalty and revenue distribution. Balochistan produces a disproportionate share of Pakistan’s natural gas and mineral wealth. The federal transfer formula has never been publicly audited in real time. The government should establish a publicly accessible, quarterly-reported Balochistan Resource Ledger, revenue extracted, royalties transferred, development funds disbursed, verified by the Auditor General and posted online. Opacity feeds the narrative of colonial extraction; transparency does not end it overnight, but it removes its most credible evidence.

Fourth, structured political dialogue, not capitulation, but conversation. Every successful counterinsurgency of the past half-century, from Northern Ireland to Aceh to Mindanao, has required, at some point, a backchannel and then a front channel with the political constituency of the insurgency, even where the armed group itself was excluded. Islamabad need not negotiate with bombers. It must negotiate with the Baloch political mainstream, the Balochistan National Party, the elected representatives who carry the grievance without endorsing the gun, and it must do so without preconditions that predefine the outcome. A constitutional amendment guaranteeing genuine provincial autonomy over natural resources should be placed on the table before the next budget cycle.

Fifth, a counter-radicalisation programme that meets the BLA’s intellectual recruitment where it actually occurs. The Majeed Brigade is now recruiting from Baloch universities and colleges. This is not a security problem that armed operations can solve; it is a contest for meaning among a generation that has watched its elders disappear and its province's wealth depart on pipelines that do not stop at local towns. The Higher Education Commission should fund a Baloch Studies programme at every major university in the province, not propaganda, but rigorous, locally administered scholarship, and the federal government should double civil service and professional appointments for Baloch graduates, ensuring that the argument of the Kalashnikov competes with a more credible argument of citizenship.

None of these measures is quick. None is costless. All of them will be resisted by constituencies that profit from the present arrangement. But the alternative, another press release, another operation in Mastung, another promise of an overhaul, another set of funerals on another Eid, has had twenty years and it has produced Chaman Phatak.

None of this absolves the men who placed a bomb beside families on their way to Eid. The BLA’s cynicism is its own. The killing of children sleeping in homes along a track they did not lay is a crime that no political grievance can launder. We must say this clearly: a movement that learns to ram explosives into a carriage carrying women and infants has forfeited the language of liberation. It now operates in the language of terror, and it should be addressed as such.

But the state too must hear the verdict of Chaman Phatak. For the families who will bury their dead this week, the formula of “cowardly act” is an insult dressed as sympathy. They are owed something better, not promises, of which there has been an excess, but the unglamorous, patient, political work the federation has refused to undertake since 1973.

The train was supposed to take them home. We failed it long before the bomber did.

Machines at War: The AI Question Nobody in Islamabad Is Asking

By Ali Hassan Sargana · April 24, 2026 · Read text

Editorial clarification · October 15, 2026

AI procurement is not proof of a particular targeting decision

NATO’s 2025 annual report confirms acquisition of Maven Smart System NATO. This documents NATO adoption, rather than the target-selection process of a particular US strike. The contemporaneous 11 March 2026 US Senate letter asked whether AI tools were used in planning or executing the Minab school strike. That letter is a request for an investigation and answers, not a finding that AI selected the school. These sources do not establish the archive’s categorical targeting claims; those require event-specific evidence.

  • NATO Secretary General’s Annual Report 2025
  • US Senate Foreign Relations Committee: 11 March 2026 letter

Original archived text

When the United States struck over a thousand Iranian targets in a single night, it was not just bombs that fell it was an algorithm that chose them. The world changed on February 28, and we are still pretending it did not.

There is a school in Minab, in southern Iran, that no longer exists. On February 28, 2026, the Shajareh Tayyebeh girls' school was reduced to rubble in what Iran says killed at least 168 people most of them school children. The Trump administration initially blamed Tehran for the strike. The US later acknowledged it was investigating. The Washington Post reported the school appeared on an American target list. Senate Democrats have since written to Secretary Pete Hegseth demanding clarity on one specific question: was artificial intelligence used to select that target? The answer to that question may well define the ethics of warfare for the next century.

The conflict that erupted on February 28, 2026 officially labelled Operation Epic Fury was, by any serious measure, the most technologically intensive war ever fought. In the first twenty-four hours alone, American and Israeli forces struck over a thousand Iranian targets. For context, the 2003 Iraq War's opening day saw roughly half that number. The difference was not simply firepower. It was the speed, scale, and precision that only an artificial intelligence targeting system could provide. According to reporting by The Washington Post, the US military leveraged the most advanced AI it has ever deployed in an active war zone to sequence, identify, and approve those strikes. Ukraine taught the Pentagon lessons. Iran became its examination.

"Our war fighters are leveraging a variety of advanced AI tools. These systems help us sift through vast amounts of data in seconds so our leaders can cut through the noise and make smarter decisions faster than the enemy can react." ~ Admiral Brad Cooper

Those are the words of Admiral Brad Cooper, head of the US Central Command, delivered in a video message in March. He was not apologising. He was advertising. And in that gap between his pride and a schoolgirl's grave in Minab lies the central moral catastrophe of our age. The system Cooper was praising is known as the Maven Smart System, an AI-enabled platform that helps soldiers identify and strike military targets, and assists chain-of-command approval for strikes. NATO acquired a version of it from Palantir in 2025. The US military is now deploying its own iteration in Iran. The human, as Cooper dutifully reassured reporters, makes the "final decision." What he did not explain is that when a human has twenty seconds to approve a strike on a location that an algorithm has flagged as a threat based on pattern-of-life data, satellite imagery, signals intelligence, and thermal signatures calling that a "human decision" is a courtesy we extend to ourselves.

Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology has been tracking these developments closely. Lauren Kahn, a senior research analyst at the centre, observed in a conversation with NPR that the US is now using artificial intelligence "across the gamut" from natural language processing to computer vision, from logistics to autonomous drone navigation. "Everything from boardroom to battlefield," she said. This is not tomorrow's war. This is today's. And yet one scans the Pakistani strategic discourse and finds almost no serious grappling with the implications. Our analysts debate ceasefire timelines. Islamabad celebrates its diplomatic role. Nobody is asking the harder question: in a world where AI selects targets and AI generates propaganda with equal fluency, what does national security even mean for a state like ours?

Consider the information dimension, which is equally alarming. The Brookings Institution documented that within hours of Operation Epic Fury's launch, social media flooded with fabricated footage: AI-generated videos of missile strikes on US warships, deepfakes of Iranian commanders celebrating victories that never happened, recycled footage from Ukraine and surreally from video games, all dressed in the grammar of breaking news. X, formerly Twitter, announced a policy to suspend accounts posting undisclosed AI-generated conflict content. As Brookings noted, this policy relies on metadata that can be removed, and community corrections that almost never come in time. The information war of 2026 is not won by the state with the better press office. It is won by the state with the better generative model.

The Iran conflict is not the first AI war, but it is the first war in which conversational AI systems were consulted not merely for analysis, but for strategic advice a paradigm shift the world's parliaments are wholly unprepared for.

MIT Technology Review, in a landmark piece published this week, made the important distinction that calling Iran "the first AI war" is technically imprecise. Algorithms have been parsing surveillance footage since Afghanistan. Ukraine pioneered the autonomous drone. Israel has long used machine-learning systems to generate target lists. What is genuinely new in 2026 is the integration of large language models conversational AI into the command chain itself. Commanders are not merely receiving AI-generated intelligence summaries. They are, in some documented cases, querying AI systems for tactical advice, the way a junior officer might consult a senior one. The AI responds. The human listens. The bomb falls. At what precise moment in that chain does accountability evaporate?

Iran, it must be noted, is not a passive object in this technological theatre. Tehran has invested substantially in its own cognitive warfare capabilities drone swarms, cyber operations, and information warfare directed at Gulf state infrastructure. In the first days of the conflict, Iranian strikes reportedly targeted AI data infrastructure in the Persian Gulf region, a signal that Iran understands perfectly well that the new front in modern warfare is the server farm, not the sandbagged trench. One must give Tehran this credit: it read the room. Meanwhile the UN General Assembly, to its modest credit, passed a resolution in December 2025 on "Artificial Intelligence in the Military Domain and its Implications for International Peace and Security." A three-day multi-stakeholder meeting is scheduled for June 2026. Discussions, consultations, best practices. While the school in Minab is already a field of rubble.

The question that Pakistan and every middle power in the Global South must now answer is deceptively simple: do we have a position on autonomous and AI-assisted warfare, or do we merely have a preference about which great power wins? Islamabad has, admirably, helped broker the current ceasefire extension. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif deserves genuine credit for threading an extraordinarily delicate needle. But diplomacy that ends a war without interrogating the technology that fought it is incomplete diplomacy.

We are negotiating the terms of silence, not the terms of the future. There are over 1,300 people dead in Iran and among them, according to Iran's health ministry, more than 400 women and children. The algorithm did not grieve for them. It is not designed to.

Pakistan sits at a civilisational crossroads that its strategic planners have not fully acknowledged. We are a nuclear state. We are a state that has experienced the full weight of drone warfare on our own tribal borderlands. We are a state that has watched, across two decades, what happens when automated targeting meets incomplete intelligence in a sovereign country. And we are now, in this emerging world order, being asked to serve as a peace broker, a swing state, a responsible actor. One cannot credibly occupy that role while declining to take a principled stand on the very technology rewriting the rules of armed conflict. The International Committee of the Red Cross has called for new international rules on autonomous weapons systems for nearly a decade. Pakistan has never led that conversation. It should.

The machines at war do not tire. They do not question orders. They do not feel the weight of a name they do not know. That is precisely why the humans who design them, deploy them, and profit from them must be held by law, by treaty, by the unrelenting pressure of the international community to account for every coordinate they illuminate in the dark. The Iran war has made one thing unmistakably clear: artificial intelligence has not made war more precise. It has made war more industrial. And industries, as history teaches with grim consistency, tend to run until someone pulls the switch.

Power Without Peace

By Ali Hassan Sargana · April 20, 2026 · Read text

THE latest phase of the US-Iran confrontation is more dangerous than its earlier forms because it has moved beyond sanctions, proxy competition and nuclear brinkmanship into direct military escalation, maritime disruption and high-risk diplomatic signalling. What was once a tense but partly contained rivalry has become a live regional crisis - and Israel's role in converting pressure into open confrontation is central to understanding why.

Pakistan’s condemnation of Israeli strikes on Iran was therefore not only a political statement; it was also an acknowledgement that the conflict had crossed into a far more combustible stage. Israel, meanwhile, has framed its actions as self-defence against an expanding Iranian threat. Between those positions lies the reality that the region is no longer dealing with controlled tension, but with a chain of moves and counter-moves in which miscalculation could prove disastrous.

One of the most significant developments in this period has been the direct US-Iran engagement in Islamabad, with Pakistan acting as host and mediator. That alone is important. It shows that even at a moment of sharp escalation, diplomacy has not fully collapsed. Yet the talks have so far produced no breakthrough. The first round was serious but inconclusive, and uncertainty still hangs over whether a second round can move forward under the shadow of widening regional insecurity.

The maritime dimension has made the situation even more fragile. Reporting around the Strait of Hormuz suggests not merely a tactical dispute at sea, but a test of economic endurance and political resolve. If passage through the strait remains obstructed, the consequences will not stay confined to the Gulf. Energy prices, trade routes and investor confidence across the wider region will all feel the impact. In that sense, Hormuz is not only a military flashpoint; it is a pressure point for the global economy.

Iran’s own position reflects a familiar but important principle in international politics: states do not negotiate only for peace, they negotiate for dignity, strategic space and regime survival. Tehran has continued to signal openness to diplomacy, but not to terms it sees as humiliating or one-sided. This is why maximalist demands are unlikely to produce lasting results. Pressure can force engagement, but it cannot by itself produce a stable settlement.

The United States appears to be pursuing a dual-track strategy diplomacy in one hand, coercion in the other. That may make sense from Washington’s perspective: it wants leverage at the negotiating table without appearing weak before allies or adversaries. But such a strategy also carries risk. If pressure is applied without a believable political off-ramp, diplomacy begins to look less like negotiation and more like managed surrender. That is precisely where talks usually harden rather than advance.

Israel, too, remains a major driver of instability in this phase. Even where temporary calm exists, it has shown little willingness to move towards full de-escalation if doing so limits its military freedom of action. That posture may offer short-term tactical advantages, but it does not create long-term stability. Military superiority can alter the battlefield; it cannot, on its own, settle the political questions that keep returning in more dangerous forms.

This is why Pakistan’s role deserves closer attention. Islamabad is one of the few capitals able to maintain channels with Washington, Tehran and other regional actors at the same time. Its value lies not in military dominance, but in diplomatic usability: geography, access, timing and a degree of trust. In a region where many actors are either openly aligned or deeply distrusted, that is not a small asset. Pakistan is no longer merely a neighbouring observer; it is emerging as a working bridge in a conflict that desperately needs one.

No side, however, can yet claim victory. Israel has demonstrated military power without delivering stability. The United States has shown coercive reach without securing a settlement. Iran has absorbed pressure without escaping heavy strategic and economic costs. Pakistan has gained diplomatic relevance, but only by carrying the burden of helping prevent a broader collapse. That is the heart of the crisis: power has been used, but peace has not been produced.

The most plausible outcome, at least for now, is not a grand bargain. It is a series of uneasy talks, partial understandings, temporary de-escalation measures and persistent mistrust. The real danger lies in a sudden miscalculation at sea, in the air, or along one of the region's volatile fault lines that pushes everyone back towards a much wider war.

A practical path forward would require all sides to move beyond rigid posturing. Washington must pair pressure with a phased diplomatic offer. Tehran must show visible flexibility without portraying every compromise as capitulation. Israel must recognise that avoiding regional expansion is itself a security interest. And Pakistan should continue to mediate, because its greatest strength in this crisis lies in keeping dialogue alive when other channels narrow or fail.

The central lesson is simple: lasting peace is rarely built through the pursuit of total victory. It is built when rival powers accept limited compromise to avoid unlimited loss. In that sense, the real winner in this crisis will not be the actor that can fight the longest, but the one that can turn power into a workable peace without appearing defeated.

The Next 48 Hours - Between Blockade and Bargain

By Ali Hassan Sargana · April 21, 2026 · Read text

Islamabad before the deadline

THE last 24 hours have stripped away any illusion that this crisis is moving along a single path. It is now advancing on two tracks at once. On one, Washington has tightened the screws: an Iranian-flagged cargo vessel has been seized, the blockade remains intact, and the White House has signalled that the ceasefire may not survive beyond the deadline. On the other, Pakistan has kept the diplomatic channel alive, and Iran, despite its hard language, has stopped short of shutting the door completely. That is what makes the coming 24 to 48 hours so consequential. They will decide whether this confrontation hardens into a wider conflict or bends, however grudgingly, back towards negotiation.

What shifted over the past day was not only the tone, but the shape of the standoff. The US interception near the Strait of Hormuz, the first such seizure since the blockade of Iranian ports began last week, gave Tehran immediate grounds to denounce the move as piracy and as a breach of the ceasefire. At the same time, shipping through Hormuz slowed again, insurers raised war-risk premiums, and oil prices climbed as markets began to price in the possibility that diplomacy was losing ground. These were not secondary tremors. They were the crisis in its clearest form: coercion at sea, anxiety in global markets, and negotiations working under pressure.

Iran’s posture, for now, is one of calculated resistance rather than complete rupture. President Masoud Pezeshkian, in his contact with Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, accused Washington of acting with bullying and unreasonable hostility and of violating prior commitments. Iranian media also reported another round of contact between the Pakistani and Iranian foreign ministers within 24 hours, a sign that Tehran still sees value in keeping Islamabad engaged. At the same time, reports that Iranian officials are positively reviewing attendance at talks in Pakistan suggest that public defiance is being paired with private flexibility. In plain language, Tehran is trying to preserve its leverage without sealing off diplomacy altogether.

Pakistan, meanwhile, has behaved with the caution of a state that understands both the opening and the danger. Shehbaz Sharif has reiterated Pakistan’s commitment to regional peace and security, while Islamabad has reportedly readied a large security deployment in anticipation of talks that may yet prove uncertain. That dual response tells its own story. Pakistan is not acting like a passive observer. It is acting like a state that knows a diplomatic collapse on its soil would carry costs far beyond ceremony. For Islamabad, the immediate task is to keep both sides near enough to the table that neither feels compelled to prove its seriousness first through escalation.

The hardest edge in this equation remains the American position. President Donald Trump has said he is highly unlikely to extend the ceasefire if there is no deal by Wednesday evening in Washington, and he has also made clear that the Hormuz blockade will remain until an agreement is reached. Reports that JD Vance had still not departed the United States, despite plans for a delegation to travel to Islamabad, only sharpen the uncertainty. Washington is trying to combine military pressure with diplomatic urgency, but there is an obvious contradiction at the heart of that strategy: the harsher the coercion, the harder it becomes for Tehran to enter talks without appearing to capitulate. The United States may believe time is strengthening its hand. In truth, time is also widening the margin for miscalculation.

The most likely immediate outcome is not a clean breakthrough, but an uneasy holding pattern. Expect intense back-channel diplomacy, louder public threats, continued Pakistani mediation, and a final effort to draw both sides into the same political space before the ceasefire clock runs down. The signals still point in that direction. Pakistan says it sees room for movement from Iran. Tehran is still talking to Islamabad at senior levels. American plans for sending a delegation have not been abandoned. China, too, has urged restraint after the ship seizure. All of this suggests that diplomacy, though diminished, is not yet dead.

The following 48 hours are far more dangerous. If talks do not begin, or if they begin without movement on the blockade, then the ceasefire deadline becomes more than a date on the calendar; it becomes a trigger. Should that moment arrive with the blockade still in place, Tehran will face growing pressure to answer the ship seizure and the broader coercive campaign with something more tangible than rhetoric. That response need not take the form of full-scale escalation. It could come through renewed maritime harassment, calibrated retaliation, or a tighter use of Hormuz itself as bargaining leverage. But each of those options would deepen the risk of a wider regional slide.

This is why Pakistan’s role over the next two days is unusually important. Islamabad cannot dictate terms to Washington or Tehran, but it may still influence the sequence of events. If Pakistan can help produce even a narrow formula talks first, de-escalation at sea second, and the blockade folded into a broader framework rather than treated as an upfront precondition - then there remains a chance of avoiding a return to open confrontation. If it cannot, Pakistan will still remain central, but in a darker sense: as the capital that witnessed the last serious diplomatic opening close in real time.

My own reading of the next 48 hours is this: Washington will keep the pressure on while preserving the option of engagement; Tehran will continue rejecting humiliation while leaving itself room for a tactical return to talks; and Pakistan will spend every remaining hour trying to prevent coercion at sea from extinguishing diplomacy on land. The most plausible outcome is a hurried and improvised contact rather than a grand settlement. The next-most plausible is not a formal breakthrough, but a messy de facto extension created by continued engagement. The worst outcome renewed military action after the deadline is still not the likeliest, but it is no longer distant. That is what makes these hours decisive.

In the end, the meaning of this moment is stark. The past 24 hours have shown that neither force nor diplomacy has prevailed. Both remain in play. The next 24 hours will test whether diplomacy can still slow the crisis. The next 48 will test whether it can still shape it. If those efforts fail, the region may wake not to clarity, but to something far more dangerous: a war paused rather than ended, a blockade still in place, a strait under shadow, and a deadline that proves to have been not a warning, but a threshold.

Why Harassers Dare - the Harasser’s Calculus

By Ali Hassan Sargana · June 12, 2026 · Read text

Editorial clarification · October 15, 2026

Read the percentages with their denominators

Female labour-force participation is the proportion of working-age women in the labour force, not women’s share of the entire workforce. The archive’s 23% wording should not be used as a workforce-share statistic without a matching source and reference year. The PDHS figure rounded to 56% refers to ever-married women aged 15–49 who had experienced physical or sexual violence and had neither sought help nor told anyone. It comes from the 2017–2018 survey; it is not a current estimate of workplace harassment or a percentage of all Pakistani women.

  • World Bank indicator: female labour-force participation
  • Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey 2017–18

Editorial clarification · October 15, 2026

Complaint records and survey prevalence are different measures

The available FOSPAH announcement about its Annual Report 2025 records 1,119 harassment complaints for 2024–2025 and separately identifies property-deprivation complaints. It reports a 375% increase in complaint registration compared with the previous year, but does not establish a calendar-2024 year-on-year series. The archive’s specific 2024 wording therefore still needs its original period-specific source. Complaint registrations measure reports received by an institution; they do not by themselves measure the prevalence of harassment among all workers.

  • FOSPAH: presentation of Annual Report 2025

Original archived text

EVERY few weeks, the cycle repeats itself. A complaint surfaces from a university campus, a newsroom, a bank, a government office. There is a brief spell of outrage, a hastily constituted inquiry committee, a quiet settlement or a quieter transfer, and then silence until the next case. The question Pakistan must ask itself is no longer whether harassment happens; every working woman in this country already knows the answer. The question is why it continues with such confidence. The numbers should unsettle us. The World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Report 2025 placed Pakistan last among 148 countries, with women constituting barely 23 per cent of the workforce. The Islamabad-based Alliance Against Sexual Harassment estimates that as many as 93pc of working women, across the public and private sectors, have faced harassment in some form. The Federal Ombudsperson Secretariat for Protection Against Harassment (FOSPAH), which received a mere 84 complaints between 2010 and 2013, recorded over 5,000 between 2018 and 2022, and reported a 375pc year-on-year surge in 2024. That climb does not mean harassment has suddenly multiplied; it means a fraction of victims have finally found the courage, and the forum, to speak. Surveys suggest only 17pc of those harassed ever approach their organisation’s inquiry committee, while the Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey finds that 56pc of women who suffer violence never seek help at all. The iceberg remains submerged.

Let us, for once, name the cause without euphemism. Behind every incident of harassment stands a man who made a choice and behind that man stands a society that taught him the choice was costless. It was never about what the woman wore, where she worked, what time she travelled or how confidently she carried herself. Criminological research is unambiguous on this point: harassment is an assertion of power, not an overflow of attraction. The man who corners a junior colleague is not overcome by passion; he is exercising a dominance he believes he is entitled to and believes he will never be made to answer for.

The psychology is well mapped. R.W. Connell’s concept of hegemonic masculinity describes how societies construct manhood around dominance, entitlement and the policing of women’s presence in public space. Albert Bandura’s theory of moral disengagement explains the rest: “it was only a compliment”, “she did not object”, “she should not have been there so late” the small linguistic devices by which otherwise ordinary men grant themselves permission. On the other side of the ledger sits the psychology of the victim: fear of stigma, fear for the family’s ‘honour’, fear of retaliation in a job market where women are already scarce. Her silence is not consent; it is a rational response to a society that punishes the complainant more reliably than the accused.

Harassment persists in Pakistan not because men cannot control themselves, but because they have calculated correctly that nothing will happen to them.

Criminology completes the picture. Shaw and McKay’s social disorganisation theory holds that deviance flourishes where informal social controls collapse and urbanising Pakistan is a textbook case: anonymous offices, unregulated transport, institutions where oversight is weak and loyalty runs upward. Wilson and Kelling’s ‘broken windows’ logic applies with equal force. Where the minor transgressions the lingering stare, the remark, the ‘accidental’ touch are tolerated, the signal travels outward that graver ones will be tolerated too.

It is tempting to respond that the remedy is education and the slow reform of society. It is also, on the evidence, insufficient. The accused in our most prominent harassment cases have not been illiterate men from the margins; they have been professors, executives, doctors and officers men carrying every degree society can confer and little of the moral sentiment those degrees are presumed to carry. Literacy is not virtue. And the reform of an entire society is the work of a generation, perhaps several, in a country where half the population stands outside meaningful schooling altogether. The woman being cornered in an office this afternoon cannot wait a generation. A strategy that rests entirely on awakening the male conscience is a strategy that has already failed.

This is where classical criminology speaks with uncomfortable clarity. Cesare Beccaria and Jeremy Bentham argued over two centuries ago that crime is a calculation: men offend when the expected gain outweighs the expected pain. Deterrence stands on three legs certainty, severity and celerity of punishment. Measured against all three, harassment in Pakistan is a low-risk enterprise. Complaints languish for years; inquiry committees protect the institution before the complainant; the social cost lands on the woman who speaks rather than the man who acted. Of the thousands of complaints processed, how many harassers’ careers have actually ended? How many names has the public ever learnt? Impunity, too, is public knowledge and it instructs.

The legal architecture already exists. The Protection Against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act, 2010 broadened in 2022 to cover students, domestic staff and the gig and informal workforce together with Section 509 of the Pakistan Penal Code and FOSPAH’s quasi-judicial powers, gives the state every instrument it needs. What is missing is enforcement that reverses the harasser’s calculus: inquiries concluded in weeks, not years; penalties dismissal, fines, imprisonment where the law provides actually imposed; and, crucially, visibly imposed. Punishment deters only when it is seen. The publicised dismissal and the reported conviction achieve what a thousand sensitisation workshops cannot: they make the next man pause. The state need not be cruel. It need only be certain, swift and unembarrassed.

To the women of Pakistan, I would say what I say to my own students. Trust your instinct; women almost invariably sense predatory intent long before it announces itself, yet are socialised to doubt themselves, to stay polite, to extend the benefit of the doubt. Do not. The first ambiguous remark deserves an unambiguous response, because the harasser is testing the waters, and he reads silence as permission. Cohen and Felson’s routine activity theory teaches that offenders act where capable guardianship is absent so make it present: object immediately, document everything, and report at once, to the inquiry committee, to FOSPAH, to the police. None of this shifts the burden onto women; the burden belongs to the man who harasses and to the state that must punish him. But reporting is the act that converts private suffering into public consequence and every complaint filed makes the next harasser’s arithmetic worse.

Institutions must do their part: inquiry committees that are not reputational fire brigades, iron-clad protection against retaliation, time-bound proceedings, and colleagues who refuse to look away. Society will, one hopes, eventually be reformed by schooling, by example, by time. Until that distant morning, fear of consequence must do the work that conscience has failed to do. The standard is simple to state: no man should dare. And when one does, the answer must arrive swiftly enough, certainly enough and visibly enough that the next one calculating, as harassers always calculate concludes that the price is too high.
