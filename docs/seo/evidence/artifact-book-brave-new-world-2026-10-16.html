Brave New World presents a future society that does not usually control people through open violence. Instead, it controls them through scientific breeding, psychological conditioning, entertainment, consumerism and artificial pleasure. Human beings are produced in laboratories and divided into social classes before birth. From childhood, they are trained to accept their assigned positions and to avoid deep thought, lasting relationships and emotional discomfort. Whenever unhappiness appears, people take a pleasure-producing drug called soma. The society looks peaceful and prosperous, but this stability has been purchased by sacrificing freedom, family, love, individuality, art, religion and serious reflection. Huxley’s central warning is that people may lose freedom not only because governments frighten them, but also because comfort and pleasure make them unwilling to resist.

  • The World State: The novel is set in a highly organized global society where political stability is treated as more important than personal freedom. Nearly every aspect of life is planned in advance.
  • Artificial reproduction: Children are produced through scientific processes instead of being born into families. The government controls their physical and intellectual development before birth.
  • The caste system: People are divided into groups such as Alphas, Betas, Gammas, Deltas and Epsilons. Each group is designed and conditioned to perform a particular social function.
  • Psychological conditioning: From childhood, citizens repeatedly hear slogans while sleeping. This conditioning makes government ideas feel natural and prevents people from seriously questioning society.
  • Soma as social control: Soma removes sadness, anxiety and emotional pain. Instead of solving problems, citizens escape from them through chemically created happiness.
  • Consumerism: People are trained to purchase new products constantly rather than repair old ones. Economic activity is maintained by turning consumption into a social duty.
  • Loss of family and love: Words such as “mother” and “father” are considered embarrassing. Stable families and lasting romantic relationships are discouraged because strong personal loyalties might compete with loyalty to society.
  • John the Savage: John has grown up outside the World State and values literature, love, religion, suffering and personal choice. His conflict with the controlled society exposes the emptiness beneath its apparent happiness.
  • Use in essays: The novel can support arguments about consumer culture, technological control, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, addiction, mass entertainment, declining individuality and the difference between pleasure and genuine happiness.
  • Quotation excerpt: “Every one belongs to every one else.” The slogan shows that the World State does not recognize deep personal relationships; individuals are treated as parts of a collective system rather than independent human beings.
